The seating chart is the task couples put off longest, and for good reason: it is the one part of wedding planning where logistics and family politics collide. It also cannot be finished early, because it depends on RSVPs that arrive late and change again in the final week.

This guide covers the whole sequence — guest list and RSVPs, table layout, who sits where and why, and turning the finished plan into printed stationery — plus the same problems as they appear at galas, banquets and corporate dinners.

## Guest Lists and RSVPs
Everything downstream depends on an accurate headcount. Tracking invitations, chasing replies, recording dietary requirements and handling the plus-ones who materialise late is unglamorous work that determines whether the seating stage takes an evening or a fortnight.

## Building the Seating Chart
Start from the room, not the spreadsheet: table count, table shapes and the physical constraints of the venue come first. From there it becomes a solvable puzzle of groups, and a drag-and-drop canvas beats sticky notes on a kitchen table.

## Etiquette: Who Sits Where
Head table or sweetheart table, where parents go, how to place divorced parents, exes and quietly feuding relatives. Most of the traditional rules exist to prevent a specific awkwardness — worth knowing before you decide which ones to break.

## Round Tables vs Long Tables
Rounds seat more people per square metre and make conversation easy in groups of eight to ten; long tables photograph beautifully and change the room's whole feel. The choice affects guest count, budget and how the evening actually flows.

## Printing Charts, Escort Cards and Place Cards
Escort cards, place cards and a seating chart poster are three different things doing three different jobs. All of them can be produced at home from a finished plan — including large-format charts tiled across ordinary sheets of paper.

## Galas, Banquets and Corporate Events
The same tooling handles sponsor tables, VIP placement, name badges and last-minute substitutions — with more stakeholders and considerably less sentiment.
