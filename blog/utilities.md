Print shops are expensive, slow, and rarely open when you need a poster tomorrow morning. Almost any home or office printer can produce large-format output — the trick is splitting the artwork across sheets correctly, which is exactly the part macOS handles badly out of the box.

This guide covers tiled printing end to end: preparing the source file, dealing with printer margins that eat your image, choosing paper sizes, and assembling the printed sheets so the seams do not show.

## How Poster Tiling Works
Tiling divides one large image or PDF across a grid of ordinary sheets. Getting it right means specifying the finished size in real units — inches or millimetres — rather than guessing at a scale percentage and discovering the error after twelve pages have printed.

## Why Printer Margins Matter
Almost no consumer printer prints edge to edge, so every sheet loses a few millimetres on all four sides. Without margin compensation those gaps accumulate across the grid and the assembled poster simply does not line up. This is the single most common reason a tiled print fails.

## Blueprints and Technical Drawings
Architectural and engineering drawings are usually vector PDFs, which means they can be rendered at full print resolution instead of being upscaled from pixels. Line weights and dimension text stay crisp at A0 if the source is treated as vector all the way through.

## Photos, Classroom Charts and Wall Art
Enlarging a photograph, printing an alphabet frieze or a periodic table, producing a wall-sized map: the same tiling workflow, with different demands on resolution and paper choice.

## Assembling the Finished Print
Trimming, overlap, registration marks and the order you tape the sheets in. A careful assembly is the difference between a poster that reads as one piece and one that obviously came out of a desktop printer.

## Comparing the Tools
Preview, Acrobat and dedicated tiling apps all claim to do this. They differ sharply on margin handling, vector rendering and whether the files ever leave your Mac.
